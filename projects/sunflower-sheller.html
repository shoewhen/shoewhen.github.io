EGR121L · SOLIDWORKS · 3D PRINTING · 2026

Sunflower
Shelling Machine

A human-operated, fully 3D-printed sheller that earned the second-highest performance score in a class of 30+ students.

MECHANICAL SYSTEM

OVERVIEW

Separate shell from kernel

For EGR121 Design Challenge 2, our four-person team had to build a hand-powered machine that cracks sunflower seeds and separates clean kernels from the shells, all within a three-minute demonstration. Scoring rewarded whole kernels recovered and penalized waste.

We split the machine into three subsystems: a hopper that lines the seeds up, a pair of geared rollers that crack them, and a sifter that sorts kernels from shells. Each subsystem went through its own rounds of prototypes. We used a morphological matrix to compare options for each function before committing to one.

TESTING THE SEED

Data before geometry

Before designing anything, we measured 50 seeds with digital calipers. They averaged 9.97 mm on the wide side and 4.89 mm on the narrow side, which set the hopper channel width and roller spacing.

We also tested how hard it is to crack a seed in each orientation by tightening a C-clamp over a seed sitting on a digital scale. Standing on its narrow edge, a seed cracked at about 5.6 kg. Lying flat, it took about 13.4 kg, roughly 2.4 times more. That result shaped the whole machine: if every seed reached the rollers on its edge, it would crack with less force and less kernel damage.

HOPPER

Getting every seed on its edge

The early hoppers were 30° funnels made of wood, taped wood, and PLA, with a slider to meter the flow. In all of them the seeds either stalled or came out too fast. A single narrow channel controlled the flow but was too slow, and a vibrating box driven by an eccentric shaft didn't shake hard enough to move the seeds.

The final hopper uses four 45° channels, each slightly wider than a seed's narrow side, spaced to line up with the cuts on the rollers. Curved guides at the bottom turn each seed upright before it reaches the rollers. The channel tops are covered with paper and tape instead of printed covers, so jams are easy to clear.

SHELLING

Seven roller prototypes

The shelling system started as a pair of PVC tubes and evolved through seven prototypes. A CAD version with built-in gears fixed the uneven rotation, but its rollers were too large and too slippery. We then tested smaller rollers with sandpaper, electrical tape, and gear-tooth surfaces. Sandpaper gripped too hard, the gear teeth crushed the kernels, and electrical tape gripped just enough.

To stop the kernels from being crushed, we cut four lengthwise grooves into each roller so the seeds are squeezed along their narrow side. After trying four groove sizes, we settled on 3 mm grooves with 20 cm of electrical tape wrapped around each roller, which lets us fine-tune the grip and the gap between the rollers.

SORTING

Simple beats clever

We first tried a 3D-printed fan to blow the light shells away, but the airflow was inconsistent. A rotating carousel sweeper with holes in its base was also built and tested, but shell pieces clogged the holes and the extra moving parts kept failing.

The final sorter is a removable sifting tray with kernel-shaped holes, sitting inside a housing above a pull-out collection chamber. The operator shakes the tray by its handles: kernels fall through and shells stay behind. The whole machine snaps together with pin joints and slots, so it comes apart quickly for clearing jams or adjusting parts.

RESULTS

Second-highest score

The machine scored +5 seeds per minute on the class scoring formula, the second-highest performance score in a class of more than 30 students. In the first round, the seeds provided were a different size than the rollers were tuned for. In the second round, feeding too many seeds at once jammed the hopper. One takeaway was to keep every intermediate prototype: having them photographed made the design process much easier to document.
